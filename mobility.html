The Persistence of Advantage

Five demonstrations on how much of a parent’s position passes to a child. They move from the ladder between generations, through comparisons across countries, to the statistical traps and the economic growth that shape what mobility looks like.

Three draw on published estimates and two on simulation. The note beneath each chart says which, and how.

I.Demonstration 1

The Mobility Ladder

Pitirim Sorokin’s Social Mobility (1927) described society as a ladder that individuals climb and descend. Peter Blau and Otis Dudley Duncan’s The American Occupational Structure (1967) turned the image into a table: where parents stood, and where their children ended up.

Each row is a fifth of parents, ranked by income; each cell shows where their children land. The rank-rank slope measures how strongly a parent’s rank predicts a child’s. Move it from zero, where origin does not matter, toward values measured in real countries.

Bottom fifth to top fifth
Stay in the bottom fifth
Stay in the top fifth

MethodProbabilities follow from a Gaussian copula with the chosen rank-rank slope. At 0.341, the slope for the United States in Chetty, Hendren, Kline and Saez (2014), the model gives 7.9% from the bottom to the top fifth and 35.8% remaining at the bottom; the published figures are 7.5% and 33.7%. At 0.180, the slope for Denmark, it gives 13.1% from bottom to top against a published 11.7% (Boserup, Kopczuk and Kreiner 2013).

Mobility is a property of the link between generations. Even where that link is weakest among rich democracies, a child born at the bottom reaches the top far less often than the one in five that chance would give.

CiteAref, F. (2026). The Mobility Ladder. The Mobility Lab. https://fariborzaref.github.io/mobility#ladder

For instructors

Goal. Read a transition table and define perfect mobility.

Ask the class to answer aloud. If parents’ income had no effect on children’s, what share of children from the bottom fifth would reach the top fifth?

  1. 0%
  2. 7.5%
  3. 20%

Answer. 20%. With no link, every origin sends one child in five to each fifth. The United States figure is 7.5%.

II.Demonstration 2

The Great Gatsby Curve

Alan Krueger, then chair of the Council of Economic Advisers, named the curve in 2012, drawing on Miles Corak’s estimates. Corak (2013) showed that countries with more inequality tend to show a stronger link between fathers’ and sons’ earnings.

Each point is a country. The horizontal axis is income inequality; the vertical axis is the intergenerational earnings elasticity, the share of a father’s earnings advantage that reappears, on average, in his son’s. Hover a point for its values, then remove the most unequal case and see whether the pattern holds.

Countries

DataEarnings elasticities: Corak (2013), Journal of Economic Perspectives 27(3). Gini of disposable income: OECD Income Distribution Database via Our World in Data, latest year available (2020 to 2023), the same figures used in the Inequality Lab. Corak pairs mobility with inequality in the parents’ generation, around 1985; recent values are used here, so the chart shows the association across countries, not its timing.

Across rich democracies, societies with wider gaps tend to pass more of them to the next generation. Fourteen countries establish a pattern; they do not establish its cause.

CiteAref, F. (2026). The Great Gatsby Curve. The Mobility Lab. https://fariborzaref.github.io/mobility#gatsby

For instructors

Goal. Distinguish a cross-national association from a causal claim.

Ask the class to answer aloud. Does this chart show that inequality reduces mobility?

  1. Yes, the correlation is strong
  2. No, it shows an association across a small number of countries

Answer. No. With fourteen cases and many differences between them, the chart describes a pattern. Establishing cause requires evidence on timing and on what else differs.

III.Demonstration 3

Generations to the Middle

The OECD’s A Broken Social Elevator? How to Promote Social Mobility (2018) translated the persistence of income into a span of time: the number of generations a family starting in the bottom tenth would need to reach the average.

Each bar is a country; the dashed line is the OECD average of four to five generations. Switch to years to see the same figures on the scale of family history.

DataOECD (2018), A Broken Social Elevator? How to Promote Social Mobility: generations needed for the offspring of a family in the bottom 10% to reach mean income, at current rates of intergenerational persistence. Selected countries; the OECD average is 4.5. Years assume thirty years per generation.

At the OECD average, closing the gap between the bottom and the middle takes more than a century. Across countries, the same climb ranges from two generations to eleven.

CiteAref, F. (2026). Generations to the Middle. The Mobility Lab. https://fariborzaref.github.io/mobility#generations

For instructors

Goal. Translate a persistence estimate into historical time.

Ask the class to answer aloud. On average across the OECD, how many generations does it take a family in the bottom tenth to reach average income?

  1. About 1
  2. About 4.5
  3. About 10

Answer. About 4.5 generations, well over a century.

IV.Demonstration 4

Regression to the Mean

Francis Galton’s “Regression towards Mediocrity in Hereditary Stature” (1886) compared the heights of parents and their adult children. The children of unusually tall or short parents were, on average, closer to the population mean; Galton put the ratio at about two thirds.

Each point is a simulated parent and child. Select the tallest or shortest tenth of parents and compare their average with their children’s. The slope sets how strongly height passes from one generation to the next; at 1, nothing regresses.

SimulatedFour hundred parent-child pairs with a mean of 68 inches and a standard deviation of 1.8 inches in both generations, values chosen to resemble Galton’s data. The slope of 2/3 is Galton’s estimate.

Some movement toward the average is a statistical certainty whenever generations are imperfectly linked. Research on mobility has to separate it from movement that opportunity produces.

CiteAref, F. (2026). Regression to the Mean. The Mobility Lab. https://fariborzaref.github.io/mobility#galton

For instructors

Goal. Recognize regression to the mean as a statistical, not social, process.

Ask the class to answer aloud. Children of the lowest-earning parents earn more, on average, than their parents did. This shows that

  1. opportunity is equal
  2. part of the rise would appear even with no opportunity at all

Answer. The second. Groups selected for extreme values move toward the mean in the next measurement whatever the society.

V.Demonstration 5

Absolute and Relative Mobility

Chetty, Grusky, Hell, Hendren, Manduca and Narang (2017) found that 92% of Americans born in 1940 came to earn more than their parents, against 50% of those born in 1984. Giving the later cohort the earlier growth rate raised the figure to 62%; giving it the earlier distribution of growth raised it to 80%.

Relative mobility asks who moves up the ranks. Absolute mobility asks whether children live better than their parents. Set the growth in typical income between the generations, then widen the children’s distribution, and watch the share earning more than their parents change while the link between ranks stays fixed.

Children earning more than their parents

SimulatedLog incomes of parents and children are normally distributed, with a standard deviation of 0.6 for parents and a parent-child correlation of 0.35. Growth shifts the children’s median; the spread setting widens or narrows their distribution. The settings are illustrative and are not fitted to Chetty et al. (2017), Science 356(6336).

Rank mobility and rising living standards are separate questions. Growth that is broadly shared lifts most children above their parents; the same growth concentrated at the top lifts far fewer.

CiteAref, F. (2026). Absolute and Relative Mobility. The Mobility Lab. https://fariborzaref.github.io/mobility#absolute

For instructors

Goal. Separate absolute mobility from relative mobility.

Ask the class to answer aloud. If incomes grow but every child keeps the same rank as their parents, absolute mobility

  1. rises
  2. falls
  3. stays at zero

Answer. It rises. Ranks are unchanged, but children earn more than their parents did.

A society can be unequal in a single year and fluid across generations, or the reverse. The study of mobility asks how much of a parent’s position becomes a child’s starting point.
